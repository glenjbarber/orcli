# 0000005 Consider tview for the interface

- Status: proposed. Two questions answered, two still open, and nothing in the
  tree has changed
- Date: 2026-10-05
- Supersedes: nothing. If it is accepted it replaces part of what 0000001 settled,
  and that relationship is stated below rather than left implicit

## Context

The reader set a gate. Whatever is chosen, all of this has to work in
Terminal.app with the default settings, and all of our own code for the three
things it involves is to be turned off entirely: copy, paste, and mouse
scrolling.

The scenario, as the reader gave it: open the program with Secure Keyboard
Entry on, ask a question, scroll back if the reply is long, copy the reply,
keep receiving input without the reading being interrupted, paste the same text
back.

Both halves of the gate are met by the tree as it stands, which is worth
saying before any library is discussed. Copy is an OSC 52 write in
`internal/clipboard`, 64 lines. Paste arrives through the terminal's own Edit
menu as ordinary keys and needs no code. The wheel works because the program
never enters the alternate screen and never draws a viewport, so the terminal's
scrollback is what scrolls.

That is the uncomfortable part. The gate was set on the belief that the tree
could not meet it, and it can. So this is not a decision about whether
something works. It is a decision about whether to replace working terminal
handling with a library.

The split matters, because "1,024 lines go" is not the cost. The cost is 1,647
lines of drawing written again against a different model, and a test suite
written entirely against the one being replaced.

## The clipboard question, answered

**Yes. `tcell.Screen` has both halves.**

```go
SetClipboard([]byte)   // write, over OSC 52
GetClipboard()         // read, and documented as something a terminal may ignore
EnablePaste()          // bracketed paste
EnableMouse(...MouseFlags)
```

Read from the module source rather than from belief, after this question was
left open in an earlier draft of this record. The belief recorded then, that a
cell grid would not implement a clipboard write, was wrong. The write half is
present, the 64 lines in the tree are duplicated by it, and the read half is
the terminal's to grant or refuse, which is exactly the Terminal.app case.

Two consequences follow, and they are not the same shape.

The tree's clipboard code can go. That is 64 lines, and the gate that motivated
this request is met by the library.

The read half does not become available. `GetClipboard` is documented as
possibly ignored, and a terminal that ignores it is not misbehaving. Paste into
the program is not that half: paste is the Edit menu sending bytes to the
process, which needs no library and no setting. So the gate's paste requirement
is unaffected by any of this.

## The frame, answered: a custom primitive

The reader chose the third of the three. The frame is a custom primitive
implementing `Draw`, which is the frame as it is today with the library's screen
underneath it.

This keeps the arrangement 0000001 settled and replaces the plumbing, which is what
the gate was about. It is also the least saving of the three, and that was said
in the earlier draft before the choice was made, so the cost stands as stated:
1,647 lines are rewritten and the custom primitive is not shorter than what it
replaces. What changes is who owns the cells, the keys, the resize and the
clipboard.

Rejected, and why, so a reader does not have to reconstruct it:

```text
twenty layout rows, each a field and a log cell   most saving, most
                                                       rearrangement
a text pane and a field pane side by side          undoes the per-row pairing
                                                       0000001 settled
```

## The receive queue, which is what makes the grid viable

The reader supplied the piece the earlier draft was missing, and it changes the
assessment rather than adding to it.

A turn delivers rows into a queue in the session rather than into the frame. The
painter drains that queue only when the reader is at the live edge, so a reader
who has scrolled back through a long reply keeps reading while rows continue to
arrive, and the rows land when the reader returns.

**The queue belongs in the session, beside the log,** guarded by the same lock.
That is the reader's answer and it is the right one for two reasons: it makes
the receive queue visibly the same kind of thing as `/queue`, since both are
deferral of something the reader is not ready for, and it puts the state where
a turn goroutine can ask about it rather than in painter-local state nothing else
can see.

Three properties follow, and each is a decision rather than a consequence.

**Rows are folded at draw time, never on arrival.** A deferred row is a whole
row, so a row that was cut to a width when it arrived would be cut again when
drawn and lose its tail twice.

**Scrolling is read, not stored.** The screen says where the viewport is, so
"scrolling" is a fact rather than a mode the program sets and has to keep in
step with. A wheel event away from the live edge means reading, and arriving at
the edge means live again.

**Nothing is dropped, only deferred.** A reader who scrolls back does not lose
the rows that arrive while they are reading. The queue is a backlog and the log
is the record, and the two together are the whole transcript.

This is the argument the earlier draft did not have. It said a grid had to
coordinate a reader's scroll position with a turn delivering rows, and that the
coordination was code the tree does not have. It does have it, once the arriving
rows go to a queue rather than to the frame, and the queue is the mechanism. The
earlier draft's claim that the custom primitive is "the least saving" was made
without knowing this, and the assessment of whether the change is worth taking
should be read again against this section rather than against the line counts
alone.

## What the change would cost that the line count does not show

**The tests.** Every test in this area draws to a buffer and asserts on bytes.
`TestTheFrameIsTwentyRows`, `TestTheLogIsNewestBesideThePrompt`,
`TestTheCaretIsNotMovedBackwards`, `TestARowWiderThanTheTerminalIsCut` and
`TestAThemeIsWrittenAsTheBase` all assert on escape sequences a cell grid does
not emit. Roughly two thousand lines of test are written against the model being
replaced. They would be rewritten rather than deleted, since what they hold is
real and worth holding.

**Terminals that behave well today stop doing so.** The primary screen with
native scrollback is a good default on a terminal with no wheel, since the
reader can scroll with the keyboard and the history is theirs. A viewport the
program owns is not that. 0000001 already gave the transcript up; this makes the
giving-up felt on a terminal with no mouse.

**Cross-compilation.** The build compiles for eight platforms with no cgo. A
cell grid brings its own platform assumptions, and `bmake crossbuild` becomes a
thing that can fail for reasons unrelated to this program. This was not
verified and is the first thing to check.

## What was not verified, and how it was checked

The library was fetched into the module cache to read its source, and `go.mod`
and `go.sum` were modified by that fetch. Both were reverted, and `git status`
reports neither as changed, so the tree carries no dependency on it. The
library is in the cache, which is outside the repository.

The claims about the library in this record are from its source. Everything
about the tree is from reading it, and the line counts are from `wc`.

## Still open

1. What do the two thousand lines of byte-level tests become, and is the
   rewrite smaller than the writing it replaces?
2. Does `bmake crossbuild` still pass for all eight platforms with no cgo?

Both are checkable before anything is written, and neither needs the decision
to have been made.

## Recommendation, offered as a recommendation

The clipboard question is the one that was going to decide it, and it came back
in the library's favour. The receive queue is the piece that makes a grid
viable, and the reader supplied it rather than it being found here.

What remains against is unchanged and is not small: 1,647 lines of drawing and
about two thousand lines of test rewritten, for a frame that has to be redrawn
either way, and a terminal that behaves well on a machine with no mouse
behaving less well.

What remains for it is that this is a terminal program and the terminal
handling is the part most likely to be wrong on a machine nobody has tried it
on. The last four decisions were about what a row carries and what a choice
writes, and none of them was about the terminal, so the record's own evidence is
that the terminal is not where the trouble has been.

Settling the two open questions first is cheap and would change the answer
either way.
